A Permanent Solution

By one offering he has perfected forever the ones he sanctified. . . . Where there’s forgiveness for sins, there’s no more sacrificing for them” (Hebrews 10:10, 12, 14, 18).

Attaching to the Vine

As Jesus and the eleven disciples walked from the upper room to Gethsemane, he gave them a picture to help them through the difficulties they would soon face. They were to think of themselves like branches attached to him as the grape vine. The Father as gardener had put that arrangement in place to accomplish his purposes in the world.

The picture had features that corresponded with what it represented. The branches were to produce fruit. To do that, they needed to stay attached to Jesus as their source of nutrients. They could not accomplish the Father’s purpose on their own. If they did not produce fruit, they would be cut out and thrown away. They were to produce fruit instead of leaves or other growths that would get pruned away; their efforts would come to nothing.

Created in Christ

“God has made us what we are. He created us in Christ Jesus for living the way he prepared ahead of time” (Ephesians 2:10).

“Create” means making something new. It implies doing something valuable, something purposeful, something people take initiative on, something they want to do

So it is appropriate for Paul to call it “creating” what God accomplished for us through the Messiah. The sacrifices that were offered before were shadows and pictures of the new reality Christ established. God valued us enough to have his own Son do his work while we were still sinners (Romans 5:8b). Jesus did not die in defeat. In Gethsemane the attackers “fell back” (John 18:6) at first when he identified himself, so their subsequently arresting him happened because he let them. Jesus did it on purpose. God took the initiative because he cared (Romans 5:8a).

Living Lord

Archaeology studies the past by examining the relics and ruins that remain. Archaeologists dig up items they use to piece together the history of peoples, places, and events all over the world. Numerous journals report their findings, and professional societies underwrite the work of the countless laborers, technicians, and scholars.

The impact of such endeavors overwhelms us. We marvel at the effort, cost, skill, and value in the discoveries. But we sense also the futility of material living as a final form of existence. The Mount of Self-Denial

From the time he first predicted his death, Jesus made clear that being his disciples called for self-denial (Matthew 16:24). He made that point in a graphic way: “pick up your cross and follow me.” When would anybody pick up a cross except on the way to their own crucifixion?

A crucial factor here is that in so doing we are following him. We are not choosing self-destruction. We are not opting for self-negation but choosing him, his values, and purposes over ours. We are heading for something rather than nothing, prioritizing one thing over another, in this case his interests over ours. In so doing, we emulate the One who entered this event saying, “Not my will but yours be done” (Matthew 26:39).

What’s the Connection?

Scripture uses shorthand statements like “Christ died for our sins” (1 Corinthians 15:3). To an outsider’s ear that may not make a lot of sense. What does crucifying “Christ” have to do with “our” “sins”? The previous verse indicates that it had to do with saving us from them. So the question tightens up: How can the death of one person connect with saving other people from their ‘sins”?

“Died for our sins” could mean that (a) he would not have had to die if we had not sinned, and that (b) he died to get rid of our sins. How does such a thing work? Sin takes place between an offender against an offended, so what can a third party do that removes an offender’s sins?
